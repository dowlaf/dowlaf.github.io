### Grand Canyon Day 1

After eating far too much breakfast ice cream and dropping off some unneeded gear at a ranger friend of a friend's house, we took the long shuttle out to Hermit's Rest to begin our hike. We started hiking at noon, just in time for the hottest part of the day.

After checking out the underwhelming Dripping Springs (the drip was slow enough that getting under it didn't cool me off much), we turned off of the Hermit Trail and onto the Boucher Trail. Purportedly unmaintained and among the hardest trailed routes rim to river, we found that the trail showed definite signs of maintenance, and the short sections requiring scrambling or route-finding quite enjoyable. The hiking along the upper layer (the Permian stratum, for the nerds among us) took us past awesome amphitheaters and sheer walls, and offered a few glimpses down canyon to the east.

As we descended through the Supai group and into the Redwall limestone, the trail hugged the edges of several cliffs which made my palms sweat and had us scramble down a few rockfalls, though thankfully neither simultaneously.

After this excitement, we contoured below cliffs for four or five miles at the same elevation before the trail found the narrow canyon which would lead us to the Bright Angel Shale layer, where we would find our first water of the day: Boucher Creek. I brought too little water from the rim, so soaking my feet and slamming ice cold water was pure bliss. 


We camped here, and I feel comfortable saying that it was one of the most beautiful campsites of my entire life. I can say with certainty that I have never backpacked anywhere like the Grand Canyon.

### Grand Canyon Day 2

Waking up at the confluence of five canyons, to the morning light on the fins, buttes, and mesas, magical. Today I thought a lot about dropping out of graduate school and moving here. I am totally in love. Our next campsite was close, about eleven miles away, so we started by exploring Topaz Canyon and Slate Canyon, both trending northeast of our campsite. Other than spotting a Peregrine falcon, the canyons were wide, dry, and unremarkable.

![a](/images/IMG_8645.jpeg)

After grabbing the packs we left by the creek, we climbed about 1,200 feet to get onto the Tonto Platform on which we would hike for the next two days. The formation of the Tonto Platform is really interesting, but I'll stop nerding out about geology. The trail through here is barren and exposed, but it more than made up for this with our first great views of the Colorado River.

The first half mile or so was a narrow granite canyon with lots of waterfalls and beautiful rock.

Once we got out of the narrows, we realized just how hot it was down so low; the nights above the rim on the AZT had been in the twenties, while tonight we would be lucky if it dropped below sixty. We stocked up on water from the very alkaline Monument Creek to avoid drinking the Colorado, which to my understanding is just as much water as it is silt, giardia, and rafter piss. We spent the rest of the day cooling off in that lovely concoction and relaxing on the beach. I got a book of Michael Chabon short stories at the South Rim library in anticipation of such down time, so I got to work on those while Bailey, characteristically, alternatively slept and ate.

![a](/images/bailey7.jpeg)

Eventually, rafters arrived, and we socialized with them for the rest of the evening. They were a very interesting bunch, ranging fifty years in age and hailing from across the US (though mostly the southwest). Many of the old dudes had floated the Grand a double-digit number of times, so they were making this trip interesting by doing technical canyoneering on their zero days, doing complicated descents involving rappels of more than three hundred feet. Sounds fun.

### Grand Canyon Day 3

The wind picked up after dark and we got sandblasted all night. Despite the nose and mouth full of sand, waking up to the light reflected off the Colorado on the rocks all around us was such a treat. 

![a](/images/bailey3.jpeg)

It never cooled off over night, so the 2,500' climb back up to the Tonto Platform was surprising grueling. We had a short day of hiking to the Havasupai Gardens, where we had planned a long side trip down the Devil's Corkscrew. I was feeling pretty sick, so I rested while Bailey went exploring.

While eating dinner, we ended up chatting with Daniel, a seeming Grand Canyon legend. He has backpacked every year in the canyon since 1975 (!!), he completed an unassisted traverse of the entire canyon in the eighties (when <30 people had done so), and has seemingly explored every nook and cranny of my map that I could ask him about. We caught him on the first night of a two month trip completely below the rim, having spent the last week burying food and water in the canyon. I don't know if he was more stoked about his trip than I was. He got a big kick when I told him I wanted to be like him when I grew up. 

### Grand Canyon Day 4

Feeling a bit better after lots of rest, we traversed our last section of the Tonto Trail to meet up with the South Kaibab Trail. Suddenly everything was blooming.

![a](/images/IMG_8704.jpeg)
![a](/images/bailey8.jpeg)
![a](/images/bailey30.jpeg)

The hordes on Kaibab trail were pretty minimal since it was still early in the morning and rim to rimmers have been stymied by closure of the north rim due to the Dragon Bravo fire which burned most of last summer. The trail is a dusty divotted mess owing to the mule trains which use it as a highway. 

![a](/images/IMG_8715.jpeg)

After enjoying the exorbitant lemonade at Phantom Ranch, we continued north before turning off on the Clear Creek Trail. After climbing back up to the base of the Tapeats layer, we hiked east along the beautifully desolate north side of the Tonto Platform. The views of the epic Zoroaster Temple and the taller but less dramatic Brahma Temple make me really want to come back to the canyon for some scrambling. 


![a](/images/IMG_8729.jpeg)
![a](/images/IMG_8740.jpeg)
![a](/images/bailey18.jpeg)

The descent to Clear Creek was definitely a highlight of the day, traversing a steep slope of brilliantly orange Hakatai Shale. You can get an idea from this photo by the creek. 

![a](/images/IMG_8741.jpeg)

We had a great evening napping, swimming in the creek, and eating dinner as the sun faded against the rocks to the east. I was reminded a few days earlier of Thomas Nagel’s essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" when Bailey mentioned the discovery, in the process of building an underwater dam to build to preserve the water table under a railroad while iron was strip mined nearby, of some kind of organism in the water table they were processing. I have loved watching the bats play each dusk, and tonight was particularly spectacular: dive bombs and flips and impossible strafes and Byzantine games of chase not cohering to my human understanding. 

### Grand Canyon Day 5

There was a ton to explore in the Clear Creek area—famous Cheyava Falls five miles upstream and a perilous descent to the Colorado downstream, to name two—but it was getting very hot in the canyon (it would hit 100 today at the bottom), I was feeling worse, and Bailey was nervous about arranging his ride to Kanab to start the Hayduke Trail in a few days' time, so we decided to exit the canyon a day early. The hike out of the Clear Creek area, across the Colorado, and up the South Kaibab Trail was uneventful. Going up the Kaibab, especially near the top, reminded me of why solitude is central to my enjoyment of the backcountry.
